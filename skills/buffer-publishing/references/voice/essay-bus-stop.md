# Essay: The bus stop (Josh, written for X, 2026-10)

Voice sample, verbatim. Long-form X essay in five parts.
Facts to check before posting: Priyanka Desai's "millennial midlife barbell" piece, the Gallup young-men religiosity finding, the Pew caveat, the British-study caveat.

1. The bus stop
I met him on a plane. Aisle and middle, strangers, the usual silence.
I had my phone out, running Termius, connected to a couple of coding agents chewing through a build, a prayer app as it happens, while I watched from a middle seat. Not the typical phone screen, and he'd caught it from the corner of his eye. Eventually he asked what I was doing. I said, oh, just coding.
He smirked. He was a coder too. Building his own app, vibe-coding his way through it the same as everyone now, and just like that we had a language. We talked the whole flight. Stacks, agents, the strange new feeling of building things by describing them. Then the plane landed and it turned out we were taking the same bus, and somewhere between baggage claim and the bus the conversation slipped past code into the things underneath it. Work, and what it's for. Life. Meaning. I told him I was a Christian. He didn't flinch and he didn't change the subject. He asked questions, real ones, and I answered them as honestly as I could.
Then the ride was ending, and we were about to become strangers again, and I asked if I could pray for him.
He said yeah. And then he asked something I've thought about ever since: what should I do?
Nothing, I told him. You don't need to do anything. Just stand there and listen. Close your eyes if you want.
So he stood there, at a bus stop, eyes closed, and I prayed. My eyes were open. I prayed through the things he'd told me over the past few hours, the girlfriend he was on his way to see, the worries he'd mentioned somewhere over the clouds without calling them worries. And I watched his face while I prayed. It went still, and then a smile.
Whether that prayer changed anything in his life, I don't know. I'll probably never know. But I know it didn't close a door.

2. What should I do?
What should I do?
I keep coming back to his question, because it wasn't a strange one. It was the most natural question in the world. It was the question we've trained ourselves to ask.
Think about what it takes to be a functioning adult right now. Nearly every good thing in life has been converted into a protocol. Health is a protocol: track the macros, hit the protein target, close the rings, score the sleep. Rest is a protocol; there are apps that grade you on it. Even not drinking is a protocol now, with counters and communities and streaks. And for people like him and me, work itself has become the purest protocol of all: describe what you want precisely enough and the machine builds it. The prompt is the work now.
Most of it is genuinely better than the toil it replaced. But live inside it long enough and it installs an assumption so deep you stop seeing it: everything good must be earned. If something matters, there are steps. If there are no steps, it probably doesn't work.
So when a stranger at a bus stop offers to pray for you, the software throws an error. What are the steps? What's my role in this? Where do I put my hands? It was as if no one had ever handed him something good and asked for nothing back.
What I told him has stayed with me longer than the prayer itself: nothing. You don't need to do anything. Just stand there.
There is almost nowhere left in modern life where that sentence gets said to a grown man. Everything speaks to him in imperatives: optimize this, track that, ship, respond, improve. Even his leisure arrives gamified. The only voices that demand nothing from him are the feeds that would happily watch him dissolve. Every voice in his life either demands something or is selling something. A prayer at a bus stop was, perhaps, the first offer in years that was neither.
That is what landed on his face: the physical experience of receiving without performing. Standing still, eyes closed, while someone else did the asking. For thirty seconds the weight of running his own life lifted.
His question is the question of a whole generation.

3. The barbell
Priyanka Desai has a sharp piece on what she calls the millennial midlife barbell. The oldest millennials are arriving at the stretch of life that has historically been the hardest, the years where possibility narrows and the life you imagined at twenty gets measured against the one you actually built. But they're arriving without the standard equipment. The houses came late or never. The marriages came late or never. The careers that were supposed to be the spine of identity are being rearranged in real time by the same tools he and I were geeking out about on the plane. A generation that optimized for a game whose rewards kept moving.
And the response, Desai argues, has split into a barbell. On one end, loud public optimization: the run clubs, the Hyrox races, protein-maxxing, cold plunges, the whole visible economy of self-improvement as identity. On the other end, quiet private retreat: small group chats instead of feeds, rituals, tinned fish dinners, sobriety, people building tiny defensible worlds. And the middle, the old impulse-driven middle of casual consumption and casual scrolling and casual everything, is hollowing out.
The two ends look like opposites. One performs in public, one hides from it. But the optimizer doesn't trust his appetites, so he installs targets over them. The retreater doesn't trust the ambient culture, so he installs walls against it. Different materials, same construction project. It's even gone chemical: GLP-1s were built to quiet the appetite for food, and there are early hints the quieting may not stop there. Desire itself, managed like a subscription.
Which is to say: what should I do? The whole barbell is that question, asked at scale, answered with protocols. And every answer on it shares the same quiet assumption as the man at the bus stop: that whatever meaning is available, you will have to make it yourself, because no one else is going to hand it to you.

4. The rich young ruler
There's a story in the Gospels about a young man with everything. He runs up to Jesus, kneels, and asks: what must I do to inherit eternal life?
What are the steps? The text says he's kept the commandments since childhood. He is, in modern terms, fully optimized. All streaks intact, every box checked, and still standing there asking, because the checking hasn't produced the thing it was supposed to produce. Mark records that Jesus, looking at him, loved him. And then gave him the one instruction that couldn't be added to his stack: sell everything, give it away, and come follow me. Not a harder protocol. The end of protocols. Stop managing your life and hand it over. And the young man went away grieving, because he had many possessions, which is to say: because he could not stop being the one in charge of his own account.
Two thousand years later, a measurable number of young men have started walking the other direction, and almost nobody predicted it. Gallup, after a quarter century of tracking American religion's managed decline, found young men's religiosity jumping sharply, for the first time overtaking young women's. Honesty requires the caveats: Pew sees no broad generational revival, the numbers are a signal rather than a wave, and a much-hyped British version partly dissolved under scrutiny. But look at where the ones who are moving actually go. Not, mostly, to the churches that tried to win them with coffee bars, light shows, and a forty-minute TED talk. They go to the heavy forms. Liturgies in old languages, fasting calendars, confession, kneeling at inconvenient hours. Everything a growth consultant would remove.
From the outside this looks like one more barbell behavior, another structure, filed next to the run clubs and the sobriety counters. But every structure in the previous section shares one invisible property: you run it. You chose it, you configure it, you can quit it, it answers to you. What these young men are kneeling into claims the opposite arrangement, and the kneeling is the tell. A liturgy does not answer to you. It was there before you arrived, it will be there after you leave, and it is entirely indifferent to your metrics. Walk far enough into it and you arrive where the rich young ruler arrived: in front of a person instead of a protocol. And the person does not offer a better list. He asks for everything.
The rich young ruler walked away sad. Some of these young men, apparently, are staying. Perhaps because they arrive with less. His problem was many possessions; this generation was never issued them. The houses didn't come, the pensions don't exist, the careers are being rearranged by machines. It is easier to surrender a life you were never able to make add up.
But following, kneeling, fasting, even surrender itself, these are still things a man does. At the center of this faith is something you cannot do at all. You can only receive it.

5. The prayer he didn't say
All of it, the protocols, the barbell, the young man kneeling then and the young men kneeling now, is one question. And underneath the question sits an assumption so deep no one thinks to say it out loud: whatever meaning is available, I will have to produce it myself.
Every answer on offer accepts that assumption. The optimizer produces meaning through discipline. The retreater produces it through curation. Even most religion, as commonly practiced, gets absorbed into it: pray more, read more, serve more, do the things. Works, all the way down. The self as both the project and the project manager, forever.
Here is what I watched break that assumption at a bus stop: a prayer he didn't say.
Intercession is the church's old word for praying on someone else's behalf, and it is the one practice on earth that cannot be done for yourself, by definition. You can meditate alone, fast alone, journal alone, optimize alone, even worship alone. You cannot be prayed for alone. It requires another person, freely choosing you, spending their own breath on your need, and your entire role is to receive it. When I told him you don't need to do anything, just stand there, I wasn't simplifying the instructions. There are no instructions. That's the whole thing. It is meaning that arrives from outside, in someone else's voice, addressed upward on your behalf, and it works precisely because you didn't produce it. It couldn't be earned, so it can't be lost. It wasn't performed, so it can't be judged. He wasn't the author, so for thirty seconds he didn't have to be the manager.
And that smile that spread across his face: it wasn't the theology landing. It was the weight lifting. Somebody else was holding his account, and above that somebody, he'd just been told, a Greater One holds it all.
This is what the rich young ruler couldn't see from inside his question. He asked what to do and grieved at the answer, because the answer was that the doing was the obstacle. The gospel's strangest claim, the one that separates it from every self-improvement system wearing its clothes, is that the decisive thing has already been done, by someone else, on your behalf. Grace is intercession at cosmic scale: another person carrying what you could not, and your role, scandalously, is to stand there and receive it. Christianity doesn't answer what should I do with a better list. It answers: it is finished. Now stop performing and come be loved.
Which is why the young men kneeling in old liturgies may be onto something deeper than they can yet articulate, and why a stranger with no religious background stood smiling at a bus stop with his eyes closed. Both had stumbled into the only room in modern life where the imperatives stop. Everything else in their world either demands or sells. Here, uniquely, they were neither customer nor project. Just the beloved, being asked for, by name.
The old word for a life built around this is communion. Not connection, which can be simulated, and not community, which can be joined like a gym. Communion is real persons carrying one another's real burdens before a God big enough to hold all of it. The church's own metaphor is two thousand years old: one body, many members, and when one part suffers, every part suffers with it.
That moment is what I've spent the past year trying to make more possible: one person hearing another's burden and praying for them out loud, by name. The app itself is just plumbing. The thing it carries is older than anything I could build.
Whether that prayer at the bus stop changed his life, I still don't know. But I know what it did for thirty seconds: it let him put everything down. Not a better answer to what should I do. Permission to stop asking.
Someone to say their name. Someone to carry it up. Someone who already has.
The prayer app is Praize. This essay is the why.
